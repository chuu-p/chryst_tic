# Plot

I want to think of the world in this story as a kind of character, that changes over the course of the story.

The world is geographically shaped like the real world, and they have the technology of the 12th century, but magic exists and there are more people alive than in the real world back then.
The nordic countries take inspiration from Freljord in League of Legends, .

Regions:

- The Nordic Countries

  - [[Syrvann]] (Sweden) - Tymo's decentralized homeland, known for wandering tribes.
  - [[Norholdt]] (Norway) - A Kingdom whose southern half (including cities like [[Brsykyr]] and [[Ofyo]]) is currently occupied by the MaxT Empire. The northern city of [[Bysfrost]] remains free but is under imminent threat.

- the decentralized merchant state, which is a collection of some city states (Amsterdam, Cologne, etc.)
- The sand and desert people, with void magic like ikathia from league
- ... many more regions, like china, japan, russia, etc.

there is no chaos energy, or similar. there are just factions and humans and stuff that are acting selfishly in their best interest, like in our real world.

## The Magic System

In this world, gods are real entities that emit specific "fields" of magic, much like the electric field in physics. Belief is the interface required to access these fields.

- **The Cult of Fayth** uses prayers as literal source code (arrays with if/else statements) to cast "golden winds of Fayth" for healing and defensive auras. The Bible is a massive program written by [[Frydemann]]. Energy is generated by belief and stored in _Frozen Hearts_.
- **The Cult of Gold** accesses the alteration field. They are purely transactional mages capable of changing materials and utilizing alteration magic.
- **The Cult of Maxt** utilizes shadow magic. Their god promotes deceit and victory at any cost. Their spells manipulate the sensory inputs and mental fortitude of their enemies, casting powerful illusions.

Each Cult has its own unique system and methodology, heavily influenced by their real-world cultural analogues.
