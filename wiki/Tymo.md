Slavoj Žižek has a pretty counterintuitive take on “mental freedom.” For him, we’re *least free* precisely when we think we’re freely choosing—because our desires are often shaped by hidden social pressures rather than explicit rules.

### The “authoritarian father” vs. “liberal father”

Žižek uses this contrast to show how power works differently:

**1. The authoritarian father**
This father says something like:

> “You *will* visit your grandmother.”

* The rule is explicit.
* You may resent it, but you clearly see the external authority.
* Crucially, you can *internally distance yourself* from it (“I’m only doing this because I have to”).

👉 Paradoxically, this leaves a kind of inner freedom intact.

---

**2. The liberal (permissive) father**
This one says:

> “You know how much your grandmother loves you… but of course, it’s your choice.”

* There’s no direct command.
* But there *is* an implied expectation and emotional pressure.
* If you refuse, you feel guilty—as if you freely chose to be a bad person.

👉 Now the control moves *inside you*. You police yourself.

---

### Žižek’s core idea of “mental freedom”

Žižek’s point is that:

* **Freedom isn’t just absence of external constraints.**
* Real unfreedom happens when **social norms and expectations shape your desires so deeply that you experience them as your own choices.**

In the liberal father case:

* You *feel* free
* But your “choice” is already structured by guilt and ideology

So you’re more controlled than under open authority.

---

### Why this matters

Žižek applies this beyond family dynamics:

* Modern societies (especially liberal-capitalist ones) often replace direct authority with:

  * soft pressure
  * “be yourself” messaging
  * moralized choice

Examples:

* “You *choose* to be productive”
* “You *want* this lifestyle”
* “No one is forcing you”

But these “choices” are heavily conditioned by culture, economy, and norms.

---

### The punchline

Žižek flips the usual intuition:

> **Being told what to do can be less oppressive than being subtly shaped into wanting what you're supposed to want.**

Mental freedom, for him, begins when you:

* recognize these hidden pressures
* question your own desires
* and see how “your choices” might not be entirely yours
